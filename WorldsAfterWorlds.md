# Worlds After Worlds
## Prologue
The thought started when I had the urge to make a modpack curated just for my group of friends. I figured it'd be a good worldbuilding practice if I could make the gameplay and/or the world generation lore-related. In my mind it was a flawless plan, write the background, choose fitting mods and let everyone have fun. I was essentially designing an experience, a whole game for them.

But I didn't want to just follow Minecraft's easy path of "medieval" and "fantasy" themes. I thought it'd be a bit too cliche, so I first started attempting a post-apocalyptic world. And since I'm ever so obsessed with religion and gods and higher forms of life, I couldn't resist myself from beginning there. 

Essentially the first idea was that the 'apocalypse' was or was caused by the fall of these primordial gods. It was a no-brainer to make them the manifestations of the biomes/dimensions themselves like the land, the sea, the nether and the end. But no matter how much time I really spent on it, it never really satisfied me where the story was leading.

I tried a lot of variations, but it was clear that even if I knew exactly what I wanted. Even if I knew what happened before the world, as the players would know it came to be, I didn't know what the world itself would be now.

Again and again I rewrote and remade the gods, the way they fell and what caused it all but I couldn't answer one question that stayed in the back of my head:
*What happens at the end of everything?*
## Cosmic Inspirations
The main sources that I drew my interpretation of the primordials was real life cosmology and the lore of the primordial monsters in adventure time. It has always intrigued me the very idea of countless monsters existing before us, before everything and before nothing. The visual image of a literal *sea of monsters* that filled every inch of a boundless void scratched a very specific spot in my brain that I just couldn't ignore. And since my mind was running along the lines of the "aftermath" of a universal event birthing something that we would call home, it lead me straight to the big bang, and the formation of our celestial bodies from the nebulae that spread across a space that knew no time. 

Since my thoughts ran along the birth of worlds and the gods representing these physical entities and vice versa, I had the very cliche idea to have the gods represent each part of the world like:
1. The land
2. The sea
3. The world below
4. The wastes
5. The forests
6. The end
It made sense (to me at least) since this meant that there would be a lore important boss for each area even if the main questline doesn't necessarily require defeating them all. And it led me to wonder how the gods fell, what they must've felt and what they thought of in their final moments. As such, I tied each god to a specific emotion that radiated so strongly in their dying breath that it caused ripple effects long after the only visible proof of their reign was nothing but bone. This felt perfect to seed the idea of the bosses that the players must fight in the present. Daydreaming about each boss being formed by a god's darkest moment and having them each represent feelings like remorse, wrath and loathing gave me a sense of renewed excitement over this project. But slowly this possibility did dim as I kept digging deeper into a made-up past.

Credits to Curious Archive and their absolutely brilliant video on [The Beauty of Games With Dead Worlds](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=58lztpgwxuk). It introduced to me the idea of "whalefall" and how a god-like being could still harbor life even after it drew it's final breaths. Whalefall is when a whale reaches the end of it's lifespan and the resulting carcass settles on the ocean floor. It's sheer size and amount of potential nutrients attract all forms of life, essentially creating a mini-ecosystem shouldered by one single death. The localized ecological home could thrive for 50 to 100 years, potentially outliving other whales that would've been alive when it all started. Isn't it beautiful? For something to have such an effect after it has lost all will and ability. 

Remembering the video and it's implications made me do a complete 180 from a dead world left barren after it's guardians fell long ago to something that emerged from the ashes that could only be compared to a phoenix's second life. I bounced the idea back and forth in my head, deciding that even if the gameplay itself isn't so different from any other fantasy pack I could make the story interesting. It led me to toy with the world generation of Minecraft for a while, testing out mods like Terralith, Geophilic etc.. I didn't really want to add any new blocks or items in this stage and therefore avoided ones like Biomes O' Plenty. I intentionally focused on just making the vanilla world more interesting to look at, even pairing some client side visual mods and resource packs like atmospherics, shine, particular and a bunch of little additions that brought more life to the terrain without making any substantial changes. 
## Less to More
Drawing a very strong inspiration from Don't starve together and Valheim, I wondered if Minecraft could also thrive from a more limited world size. After some time spent on research and scrolling through mod pages every chance I get, I came across a mod called WorldGenEditor. It allows you to apply presets like an island, archipelagos and such but also lets you edit the .json file directly to yield custom world generation shapes. It was centered around the idea of having limited land space that's surrounded by an "ocean biome" (which you can also change to whatever biome you desire) paired with it's own little visual preview applet that reeled me in immediately. Without much thought on what it meant for the lore, I decided that the progression would stay on a main island that harbored all the structures, loot and resources the players might need. I spent a good few days pairing other world generation mods with my custom island, adding some vanilla friendly structures to the overworld as well. 

I swear the WorldGenEditor had a spell on me because my mind completely zeroed in on designing the overworld and overworking my poor RTX 2050 laptop to churn out a full map using Xaero's world map mod which meant pre-generating every chunk of land that the players would set foot on. After toiling away with my computer at this, I finally sat back down and thought about the story again. If you haven't noticed, I basically put the lore thoughts on hold the entire time and tried to fit it back into the new world like a corporation referencing a decade old meme to appeal to the 'youth'. Much like the example, it just didn't work. 

Since my initial idea locked the gods to the land, the sea and so on, it meant that well.. the land didn't really have much to work with while the sea would be noticeably overpowering. I didn't like the thought of a power imbalance between the deities mainly because I never really wanted to powerscale them from the start. They were gods; they were always meant to be something we couldn't and should never attempt to measure by our own puny standards.
